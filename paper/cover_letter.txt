Dear Editor-in-Chief and Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, “When More Features Hurt: Feature-Synthesis Variance Amplification in Automated Feature Engineering,” for publication as a research article in Knowledge-Based Systems.

The manuscript addresses a practical and theoretical problem in automated machine learning: whether expanding the feature space improves reliable generalization when training data are imperfect. We present AutoFE-ShiftBench, a scope-aware benchmark covering 25 configured OpenML tabular data sets, with confirmatory analysis on the 22 data sets having substantial completed coverage. The benchmark evaluates seven feature pipelines, ten classifiers, five seeds, five folds, and fourteen training-data conditions, yielding 538,972 completed evaluation records in the primary scope.

The central contribution is Feature-Synthesis Variance Amplification (FSVA), a mechanistic framework explaining why unrestricted feature synthesis can degrade performance. The manuscript formalizes four linked mechanisms: perturbation propagation through feature operators, estimator-variance inflation under expanded representations, redundancy and effective search-space growth, and the bias–variance trade-off created by feature-space expansion. We then test the resulting predictions using held-out ROC–AUC, train–test gaps, condition-wise robustness, feature complexity, and computational cost.

The results support a bounded but important conclusion. In the completed benchmark scope, capped raw-feature pipelines consistently outperform unrestricted synthesis under controlled training-data corruption. Addition/subtraction-only synthesis remains close to the raw-feature reference, whereas broader synthesis variants show lower ROC–AUC, larger train–test gaps, and higher feature-generation cost. These findings do not imply that automated feature engineering is universally harmful. Instead, they show that AutoFE claims should be evaluated by feature stability, operator scope, robustness, failure accounting, and computational utility rather than by feature count alone. This perspective is directly relevant to the design of reliable, knowledge-based and AutoML systems.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration by another journal, and has been approved by all authors. The authors declare no competing financial interests or personal relationships that could have influenced the work. The study uses public tabular benchmark data and does not involve human participants or newly collected personal data. The source code, manuscript source, bibliography, and canonical PDF figures are available at:

https://github.com/mdshoaibuddinchanda/AutoFE-ShiftBench

The manuscript also reports its scope limitations explicitly, including incomplete coverage for stopped long-running data sets, training-only corruption semantics, incomplete provenance for historical checkpoints, and the need for data-set-level confirmatory inference. We believe this transparent treatment will be valuable to readers interested in robust feature engineering, data-centric machine learning, and reproducible knowledge-based systems.
