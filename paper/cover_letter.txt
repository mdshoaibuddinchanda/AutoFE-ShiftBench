Dear Editor-in-Chief and Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, “When More Features Hurt: Feature-Synthesis Variance Amplification in Automated Feature Engineering,” for publication as a research article in Knowledge-Based Systems.

Automated feature engineering is now a standard component of modern AutoML systems, yet its robustness under imperfect training data remains poorly understood. This manuscript combines a large-scale empirical benchmark with a mechanistic explanation of when automated feature synthesis ceases to improve generalization. The study evaluates seven feature-engineering pipelines and ten classifiers across 22 public tabular data sets with substantial completed coverage and more than 538,000 completed evaluation records.

The central contribution is Feature-Synthesis Variance Amplification (FSVA), a unified framework explaining why unrestricted feature synthesis can degrade performance. FSVA formalizes four linked mechanisms: perturbation propagation through feature operators, estimator-variance inflation under expanded representations, redundancy and effective search-space growth, and the bias–variance trade-off created by feature-space expansion. To our knowledge, this is among the first benchmark studies to evaluate automated feature engineering under controlled training-data corruptions while connecting the empirical findings to a unified theoretical account.

The results support a bounded but important conclusion. In the completed benchmark scope, capped raw-feature pipelines consistently outperform unrestricted synthesis under controlled training-data corruption. Addition/subtraction-only synthesis remains close to the raw-feature reference, whereas broader synthesis variants show lower ROC–AUC, larger train–test gaps, and higher feature-generation cost. These findings do not imply that automated feature engineering is universally harmful. Instead, they show that AutoFE claims should be evaluated by feature stability, operator scope, robustness, failure accounting, and computational utility rather than by feature count alone. This perspective is directly relevant to the design of reliable, knowledge-based and AutoML systems.

We believe the work is particularly well aligned with Knowledge-Based Systems because it advances robust knowledge discovery, interpretable feature construction, and reliable machine-learning systems through both theoretical analysis and extensive empirical validation. The manuscript is original, is not under consideration by another journal, and has been approved by all authors. The authors declare no competing financial interests or personal relationships that could have influenced the work. The study uses public tabular benchmark data and does not involve human participants or newly collected personal data. The manuscript reports its scope limitations explicitly, including incomplete coverage for stopped long-running data sets, training-only corruption semantics, and the need for data-set-level confirmatory inference. We believe this transparent treatment will be valuable to readers interested in robust feature engineering, data-centric machine learning, and reproducible knowledge-based systems.
